## F1-1. How an F1 car is sucked onto the track (Omnira F1)

An F1 car is sucked onto the track. Here's how.

Air rushes under the car and gets squeezed between the floor and the road. Squeezed air speeds up, and fast air has low pressure.

So the normal air pressure on top pushes the whole car down.

The trick is getting that fast air out at the back. That's the diffuser: the ramp under the rear that lets the air slow down and spread out, and pulls even more air through underneath.

Bigger diffuser, more suction, more grip.

In 2009, one team found a way to fit two. And it won them the world title.

## F1-2. Why F1 cars throw sparks (Omnira F1)

Ever wondered why F1 cars throw sparks?

Under every car there's a plank, running down the middle of the floor. Set into it are blocks of titanium.

When the car is squashed down at high speed, those blocks scrape the track, and titanium throws spectacular sparks.

But the plank has a second job. It's a ruler. Teams want to run the car as low as possible, so the plank is measured after the race.

Wear it down too much, and you're out. In 2023, two cars were thrown out of the United States Grand Prix for exactly that. One of them was Lewis Hamilton's.

## F1-3. The six-wheeled F1 car (Omnira F1)

This Formula One car had six wheels. And it won.

It was the Tyrrell P34. Four tiny wheels at the front, two normal ones at the back.

The idea was simple. Small front wheels hide behind the front wing, so there's less drag. Four of them give more grip than two.

In 1976, at the Swedish Grand Prix, the six-wheelers finished first and second.

But the tiny front tyres were made just for this one car. When development of them stopped, the advantage disappeared.

And a few years later, the rules said four wheels only. It never came back.

## FB-1. The keeper who danced a final away (Omnira Football)

This is how a goalkeeper danced his way to a European Cup.

Rome, 1984. Liverpool against Roma, in Roma's own stadium, and it goes to penalties.

Liverpool's keeper, Bruce Grobbelaar, wobbles his legs on the line like they're made of spaghetti. Roma miss twice. Liverpool win.

Twenty-one years later, Istanbul. Liverpool against Milan, and it goes to penalties again.

Jamie Carragher tells his keeper, Jerzy Dudek, to do the same.

So he does. Dancing, waving, wobbling.

Milan miss three. Liverpool win. Same trick, same club, same trophy.

## FB-2. Two goals in a final, for the wrong team (Omnira Football)

Did you know this man scored twice in a Champions League final, while on loan?

Hernán Crespo, Istanbul, 2005. Two goals for AC Milan before half-time. Three–nil up.

But Crespo wasn't really a Milan player. He was on loan from Chelsea.

And who had knocked Chelsea out in the semi-final? Liverpool. The team he was now beating three–nil.

You probably know what happened next. Liverpool came back to three–three, and won on penalties.

Crespo's two goals in a final weren't enough. And he went back to Chelsea.

## FB-3. The champions who had to start from the very bottom (Omnira Football)

This club won the Champions League and wasn't allowed to defend it.

Liverpool, 2005. They'd just won in Istanbul. But they finished fifth in the Premier League, outside the places for next season.

So under the rules, the champions of Europe weren't in the competition.

UEFA made an exception. Liverpool could defend their title, but they had to start in the very first qualifying round, in July.

Their first opponents? The New Saints, from the Welsh league.

Liverpool won both games, three–nil. And after that, UEFA changed the rules, so the holders always get the chance to defend it.
