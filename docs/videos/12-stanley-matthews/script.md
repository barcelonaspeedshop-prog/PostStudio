## Cold open

[Library: 1950s muddy match, black and white. Then F3: a grey-haired man lacing old leather boots in a 1960s dressing room. Label: AI reconstruction.]

On the sixth of February 1965, a man walked out to play in England's top division.

He was fifty years old.

A month earlier, the Queen had made him a knight. He was the first footballer ever knighted while he was still playing.

Nine years before that, he'd won the very first Ballon d'Or. He was forty-one.

His name was Stanley Matthews. Pelé said he was the man who taught the world how football should be played.

He played through a world war. He was sold because he was too old. He lost two Cup finals, and then won the most famous one ever played, at thirty-eight.

And he did it all for a few pounds a week.

This is the story of the man who refused to get old.

## Chapter 1: The barber's son

[Title card: CHAPTER 1, THE BARBER'S SON. F1: 1920s Potteries, bottle kilns, smoke, terraced streets. F2: a barber's shop with boxing posters. Fact card: born 1 February 1915, Hanley.]

Stanley Matthews was born on the first of February 1915, in Hanley, one of the six towns of Stoke-on-Trent.

This was the Potteries. Hundreds of bottle-shaped kilns, smoke over everything, and streets of small terraced houses where men worked long shifts making the country's plates and cups.

His father, Jack, ran a barber's shop. He was also a boxer, known around Hanley as the Fighting Barber. He was strict, he was fit, and he wanted his son in the ring.

But Stanley's passion wasn't boxing. It was football.

He'd dribble a ball through the streets on his way to school. Later he'd say he learned to beat defenders by beating lamp-posts, kerbs and anyone who stood still long enough.

On his fifteenth birthday, Stoke City took him on as an office boy, for a pound a week. He did odd jobs around the ground, and trained with the professionals whenever he could.

On his seventeenth birthday, they signed him as a professional.

Seven weeks later, in March 1932, he made his debut, away at Bury.

Even as a teenager he earned the maximum any footballer in England was allowed: five pounds a week. His father made him save it.

Remember that. Discipline. It's the thread through everything that follows.

## Chapter 2: How he beat you

[Title card: CHAPTER 2, HOW HE BEAT YOU. Drawn explainer on a pitch diagram (not AI): the winger, the full-back, the touchline; arrows build step by step. F9 between steps as illustration.]

So what made Matthews different?

He played outside right, the winger who hugs the touchline on the right of the pitch. And he did one thing better than anyone alive. He beat the man in front of him.

Here's how.

[Step 1 on the diagram.]

He'd walk the ball up to the full-back. Slowly. Almost too slowly. He'd stop, a yard or two away, with the ball right at his feet.

Now the defender has a problem. If he dives in, he might miss. If he waits, he's letting Matthews decide.

[Step 2.]

Then Matthews leaned. A drop of the shoulder, inside, towards the middle of the pitch. The body says "I'm going this way". The full-back's weight goes with it.

[Step 3.]

And in that half a second, Matthews was gone. Outside, down the line, with a burst over the first ten yards that nobody could match.

[Step 4.]

Then the part defenders hated most. He didn't cross early. He ran to the goal line, almost to the corner flag, and pulled the ball back across the six-yard box, to a team-mate running in, facing the goal.

From there, the goalkeeper is going the wrong way, and the defenders are running back towards their own net.

Full-backs knew exactly what he was going to do. They'd been told all week. They still couldn't stop it.

That's why they called him the Wizard of the Dribble.

## Chapter 3: The town that wouldn't let him go

[Title card: CHAPTER 3, THE TOWN THAT WOULDN'T LET HIM GO. Fact card: England debut 1934, scored v Wales. Then AI period newspaper: STOKE STAR ASKS TO LEAVE. F-new: a packed 1930s public hall meeting.]

By his early twenties, Matthews was playing for England. He won his first cap in 1934, and in only his second game, against Wales in Cardiff, he scored.

But at Stoke, things weren't going as well as he'd hoped. In February 1938, after a falling-out with the manager, he asked for a transfer.

What happened next tells you what he meant to the town.

More than three thousand people packed into the King's Hall in Stoke for a meeting with one aim: keep Stanley Matthews. Another thousand stood in the streets outside because they couldn't get in.

The local pottery owners joined in. Some of them said their workers were so upset, production in the factories had dropped.

Stoke turned the request down. Matthews stayed, and wrote to the local paper to thank the fans.

He was twenty-three, and a whole industrial town had almost gone on strike to keep him.

## Chapter 4: Berlin

[Title card: CHAPTER 4, BERLIN. Real photos only where they exist; otherwise an AI wide of a 1930s Olympic stadium, no swastikas generated (paint out if any appear). Label: AI reconstruction.]

Three months later, Matthews was in Berlin.

May 1938. England were playing Germany in the Olympic Stadium, in front of more than a hundred thousand people and the leaders of the Nazi government.

Before kick-off, the England players were told they would have to give the Nazi salute.

Matthews later wrote that the whole team was livid. The captain refused. Then an order came from the British ambassador, backed by the Football Association: the political situation was too sensitive. Do it.

They did it. The photograph went round the world, and it still follows that team today.

Then they went out and won six–three. Matthews scored one of the six.

Fifteen months later, the war began.

## Chapter 5: Too old at thirty-two

[Title card: CHAPTER 5, TOO OLD AT 32. Fact card: RAF corporal; 29 wartime internationals, no caps. Then F4: Blackpool beach at dawn, a lone man running at the water's edge.]

The war took six years out of the middle of his career.

Matthews joined the RAF and became a corporal, based near Blackpool. He played whenever he could, as a guest for Blackpool and other clubs, and he played twenty-nine games for England.

None of them counted. Wartime games weren't official, so there were no caps.

When peace came, he was thirty. Most wingers were finished at thirty-two.

And this is where the discipline started to matter more than the talent.

He never smoked. He hardly drank. He ate an almost vegetarian diet, and he fasted one day every week.

He was up at dawn, on the beach at Blackpool. Running. Sprints. Breathing exercises. All before most of his team-mates had got out of bed.

Team-mates thought he was eccentric. Nobody called it sports science, because the phrase didn't exist.

In 1947, Stoke sold him to Blackpool, for eleven and a half thousand pounds. He was thirty-two.

Plenty of people in Stoke thought the club had done well to get anything for him.

## Chapter 6: The final he couldn't win

[Title card: CHAPTER 6, THE FINAL HE COULDN'T WIN. F5: Wembley twin towers, crowds outside. Scoreboards: 1948 Blackpool 2–4 Manchester United; 1951 Blackpool 0–2 Newcastle. Then the 1950 World Cup and 1953 Hungary as quick fact cards.]

At Blackpool, he got to the FA Cup final.

In 1948, they lost it, four–two, to Manchester United.

That same year, the football writers created a new award, Footballer of the Year. And who was its first winner? Stanley Matthews.

In 1950, England went to their first World Cup, in Brazil. Matthews was in the squad. But he'd arrived late, after a tour with another England party, and the selectors kept him back for the bigger games.

So he was sitting with the reserves when England lost one–nil to the United States. One of the biggest shocks the game has ever seen.

In 1951, Blackpool reached the Cup final again, but once again they lost, this time to Newcastle.

Matthews was thirty-six. The FA Cup was the one trophy every English player wanted, and the country had started to accept he was never going to win it.

## Chapter 7: The Matthews Final

[Title card: CHAPTER 7, THE MATTHEWS FINAL. The big one: build it slowly. F6: a 1953 front room, a family round a tiny television, curtains drawn on a sunny afternoon. Then F8: Wembley, 100,000.]

Two years later, Blackpool reached the final for a third time.

Saturday, the second of May 1953. Wembley. One hundred thousand people inside.

And something new.

That spring, families all over Britain had bought or rented a television, ready for the Queen's coronation a month later. Neighbours who didn't have one crowded into the front rooms of those who did, curtains drawn against the afternoon sun.

So millions of people were about to watch a Cup final live, at home, for the first time.

What they saw first was a disaster.

[Graphic: the clock and the score build minute by minute, on our animated pitch.]

After seventy-five seconds, Bolton's centre-forward, Nat Lofthouse, scored. One–nil.

Blackpool's Stan Mortensen equalised before half-time. But Bolton scored again. Two–one at the break.

Ten minutes into the second half, Bolton's Eric Bell made it three–one. And he scored it while playing on a torn hamstring.

Three–one. Thirty-five minutes left.

Matthews was thirty-eight. He'd lost two finals already. And it was happening again.

[F9: the right wing, a 1950s winger with the ball, the full-back backing off. AI reconstruction.]

So Blackpool did the only thing left. They gave him the ball. Every time.

He went at the left side of Bolton's defence, again and again. Walk up. Lean in. Go outside.

With twenty-two minutes left, he crossed, and Mortensen bundled it in. Three–two.

The minutes went. Eighty. Eighty-five. Eighty-eight.

[P2: Mortensen's free-kick. Premium clip, start and end frames. AI reconstruction.]

In the eighty-ninth minute, Blackpool won a free-kick, twenty yards out.

Mortensen hit it, hard and high, into the top corner. Three–three.

It completed his hat-trick. It's still the only hat-trick ever scored in a Cup final at the old Wembley.

Extra time looked certain.

[P1: the winner. Premium clip, start and end frames. AI reconstruction.]

And then, in stoppage time, Matthews got the ball one more time, out on the right.

He went past his man. He reached the goal line. And he pulled the ball back, exactly the way he'd done it a thousand times.

It ran behind Mortensen, to Bill Perry, arriving late at the far side.

Four–three.

[F10: Wembley crowd erupting. F7: the same 1953 front room, the family on its feet.]

Wembley went mad.

In front rooms all over the country, people who'd never seen a live match leapt off their settees.

Mortensen had scored three. Perry had scored the winner. But from that day to this, it's been called the Matthews Final.

## Chapter 8: The oldest best player in Europe

[Title card: CHAPTER 8, FORTY-ONE. Fact card: 25 November 1953, England 3–6 Hungary, Wembley. Then the Ballon d'Or graphic.]

Six months later, he was back at Wembley, on the right wing for England, against Hungary.

Hungary hadn't lost in years. They passed the ball in triangles England had never seen, with players swapping positions all over the pitch.

They won six–three. Matthews and Mortensen both played. English football was never quite as sure of itself again.

But Matthews kept going.

In 1956, a French football magazine invented a new award for the best player in Europe. They called it the Ballon d'Or.

And once again, who was the first winner of this new award? Stanley Matthews.

He was forty-one.

No player that age has won it since. Nobody has come close.

He played his last game for England in May 1957. He was forty-two years and a hundred and four days old. He's still the oldest man ever to play for England.

## Chapter 9: Coming home

[Title card: CHAPTER 9, COMING HOME. F11: 1961 crowd queuing outside a northern ground. Graphic: a counter climbing to 35,974. Library lib01_14: fans walking to the ground at dusk.]

In 1961, he was forty-six. Blackpool let him go.

Stoke City were near the bottom of the Second Division, playing in front of small crowds. They paid three and a half thousand pounds to bring him home.

For his first game back, almost thirty-six thousand people turned up. More than three times the crowd at the game before.

Two seasons later, Stoke were champions of the Second Division, and back in the top flight. In the last home game, against Luton, Matthews scored the goal that sealed it.

He was forty-eight. The football writers voted him Footballer of the Year again, fifteen years after they'd first given it to him.

## Chapter 10: Fifty

[Title card: CHAPTER 10, FIFTY. Library lib01_13: the 1970s dressing room, slow push-in. Fact card: knighted 1 January 1965; last game 6 February 1965, aged 50 years and 5 days.]

On New Year's Day 1965, he became Sir Stanley Matthews. No footballer had ever been knighted while still playing. None has since.

Five weeks later, on the sixth of February, five days after his fiftieth birthday, he played his last game in the First Division.

He's still the oldest man ever to play in England's top flight.

[F-new: a 1965 stadium at night under floodlights, packed. Fact card: 28 April 1965, about 35,000; Yashin, Puskás, Di Stéfano, Eusébio.]

But Stoke weren't letting him go quietly.

On the twenty-eighth of April 1965, around thirty-five thousand people came to his farewell match. The opposition was a World Eleven: Lev Yashin in goal, Ferenc Puskás, Alfredo Di Stéfano, Eusébio.

The greatest players on earth had come to Stoke-on-Trent to say goodbye to him.

At the end, they carried him off the pitch on their shoulders.

In thirty-three years of professional football, he was never sent off. Most accounts say he was never even booked.

## Epilogue: Stan's Men

[F12: a 1970s township pitch, boys playing barefoot. F13: Stoke streets lined with people, 2000. Pelé quote on screen.]

He didn't stop.

He managed Port Vale, for a while without pay. He coached all over the world.

In 1975, in apartheid South Africa, he set up a team of black schoolboys in Soweto. They called themselves Stan's Men. In South Africa at that time, that alone was a statement.

He kept fit into his seventies and his eighties.

Sir Stanley Matthews died in February 2000, aged eighty-five.

On the day of his funeral, more than a hundred thousand people lined the streets of Stoke-on-Trent.

Pelé said he was "the man who taught us the way football should be played".

And the boy who beat lamp-posts on his way to school had played professional football for thirty-three years.
