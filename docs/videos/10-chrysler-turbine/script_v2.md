## 1. Cold open

In 1963, Chrysler did something no car company had ever done, or probably even contemplated.

It built fifty cars with jet engines. Then it handed them to ordinary American families, for free, to use every day.

To go to work. To do the school run. To go shopping.

Two hundred and three families drove them for three years, more than a million miles.

And then Chrysler took almost all of them back, and destroyed them.

This is the story of the Chrysler Turbine Car.

## 2. The jet age

In the early sixties, America was obsessed with the jet age.

Airliners had gone from propellers to jets. Fighter planes broke the sound barrier. Everything new had fins, and chrome, and the word "rocket" somewhere on it.

And Chrysler's engineers had been working on a gas turbine for cars since the forties.

They believed it was the future. One day, they thought, every car would have one.

## 3. How a turbine drives a car

[Animated cutaway of the engine: each part lights up as it's named, air flowing through in blue, turning red as it heats.]

So how do you drive a car with a jet engine?

First, air comes in at the front, and a compressor squeezes it.

Then fuel is sprayed in and burned, in a single chamber. A constant flame, like a blowtorch.

The hot gas rushes out, and spins a turbine. That first turbine only drives the compressor, and keeps the engine running.

[The second turbine lights up, then the driveshaft, then the wheels.]

Then the gas hits a second turbine, and this one isn't connected to the first one at all. It's connected to the wheels.

So the faster the gas flows, the harder the wheels are pushed.

And there was one more trick. Jet engines throw away a lot of heat out of the back. So Chrysler fitted two big spinning discs, called regenerators. They soaked up the heat from the exhaust and passed it back to the air coming in.

[The regenerator discs turn slowly, glowing as they pick up heat from the exhaust side.]

The result was an engine with around eighty per cent fewer moving parts than a normal V8.

No radiator. No antifreeze. No warming it up on a cold morning.

## 4. It would run on anything

And because it just burned a constant flame, it didn't care what you fed it.

[Fuel cans drop in one by one: diesel, kerosene, jet fuel, unleaded.]

Diesel. Kerosene. Jet fuel. Unleaded petrol. Even, they said, perfume.

The story goes that when the President of Mexico was shown one, he asked if it would run on tequila. That hadn't been tried yet, but they filled one up with Mexican tequila, and it worked.

## 5. The car

For the body, Chrysler went to Italy. The coachbuilder Ghia built fifty-five of them by hand.

They were painted a special colour, Turbine Bronze. Bronze on the outside, bronze leather on the inside.

The design was by Chrysler's Elwood Engel, and the jet theme was everywhere. The headlights, the rear lights, even the centre console, looked like they belonged on a plane.

[Close-ups: the turbine-shaped wheel centres, the rear lights, the jet-style console.]

And it sounded like nothing else on the road. Not the usual V8 roar that most cars gave out at the time. The turbine car was more like a jet on the runway.

## 6. The experiment

But here's the part that really made the turbine car famous.

Instead of just showing it at motor shows and selling them to the lucky few, Chrysler gave fifty of them to the public.

More than thirty thousand people wrote in, asking to take part.

Chrysler picked two hundred and three drivers, from all over America. Each family got a car for three months. Chrysler paid for the insurance and the servicing. All they paid for was the fuel.

[A map of America, dots lighting up across the country as the cars arrive.]

They drove them to work, to church, on holiday. Neighbours came out to stare. Some drivers said they couldn't fill up without a crowd forming round the pump.

Between them, they drove more than a million miles.

## 7. What the families found

So what was it actually like?

Most of the drivers loved it. It was smooth, almost no vibration. It started first time on cold mornings. It hardly ever needed servicing.

But there were problems.

[A throttle pedal pressed, a delay, then the needle rising.]

Press the accelerator, and there was a pause before anything happened, while the turbine spun up. At a junction, that could feel like a long time.

It wasn't economical, either. It used much more fuel than an ordinary car, and at high altitude, some cars wouldn't start properly.

None of it was fatal. Chrysler's engineers fixed what they could, and the next engine was better. But it was clear the turbine wasn't ready yet.

## 8. The end

In January 1966, the experiment ended. The families gave the cars back.

And then Chrysler did something that still upsets car fans today.

It destroyed them.

Of the fifty-five cars built, forty-six were crushed or burned.

[A row of Turbine Bronze cars, then an empty space where they stood.]

Why? The usual explanation is tax. The bodies had been built in Italy and brought into America, and the import duty had never been paid. Keeping the cars meant paying it.

Some say Chrysler also didn't want hundreds of strange, hand-built cars out on the road with no spare parts and no mechanics who understood them.

Either way, the jet-age dream went into the crusher.

## 9. The survivors

But not quite all of them.

Nine Turbine Cars survived. Chrysler kept two. Most of the rest went to museums.

And one of them ended up with the TV star and car nut Jay Leno.

[Fact card: the surviving cars and where they are.]

Chrysler kept working on turbines for years afterwards, with newer and better engines.

But at the end of the seventies, Chrysler nearly went bust. The government rescued it with a huge loan, and the turbine programme was one of the things that ended.

A car that would run on tequila never went on sale.

## 10. What if

So here's the question.

Was the turbine car a brilliant idea that was killed too soon? Or was Chrysler right, and it was never going to work?

Tell me what you think in the comments.

## Fact notes (for us, not for reading)

- **The cars:** 55 built in 1963–64 (5 prototypes plus 50 for the user programme), bodies hand-built by Ghia in Turin and finished in Turbine Bronze (a frost bronze), designed under Elwood Engel.
- **The programme:** October 1963 to January 1966. 203 users, about 3 months each. More than 30,000 applications (widely reported). Users paid only for fuel. About 1.1 million miles in total.
- **The engine:** A-831 twin-regenerator gas turbine, about 130 hp and very high torque at stall. About 80% fewer moving parts than a piston V8 (Chrysler's claim, widely quoted). Multi-fuel: diesel, kerosene, jet fuel, unleaded. Leaded petrol wasn't recommended, as it fouled the regenerators. Keep "they said" for the perfume line.
- **The tequila story:** widely reported (a Mexican president and tequila); keep it as "the story goes".
- **The problems:** throttle lag, fuel economy, and starting problems at high altitude (all reported in Chrysler's own programme summary and in later histories).
- **The end:** 46 destroyed. Import duty is the usual explanation; say "the usual explanation". 9 survive: Chrysler kept 2, museums have the others (engines often removed), and Jay Leno owns one (bought from Chrysler in 2009).
- **Afterwards:** Chrysler kept developing turbines into the late 1970s/1980s; the 1979–80 federal loan guarantee led to the programme ending. Keep it general ("one of the things that ended").
- **No death or injury content anywhere.**
