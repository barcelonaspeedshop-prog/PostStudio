# BANNED: The Disgrace of Gijón

Script for narration, about 800 words, roughly 6 minutes at a relaxed pace.

## 1. Cold open  [0:00]

In 1982, two of Europe's best football teams played a World Cup match that, after ten minutes, nobody seemed to want to win.

They passed the ball sideways. They passed it backwards. And when they did shoot, it went nowhere near the goal.

The crowd waved money at them. A television commentator told his viewers to switch off.

And the team that paid the price wasn't even on the pitch.

What happened in Gijón was so shameful that football had to change its rules — so it could never happen again.

## 2. The underdogs  [0:40]

Spain, 1982. Algeria are at their first ever World Cup.

Their first opponents? West Germany. The champions of Europe.

The Germans had won every one of their qualifying games. Eight out of eight. Thirty-three goals scored, three conceded.

Their coach, Jupp Derwall, reportedly said that if they lost to Algeria, he would jump on the first train home.

Sixteenth of June. Gijón.

Algeria didn't sit back and defend. They attacked. Fast, one-touch football — and the Germans couldn't cope.

Early in the second half, Rabah Madjer put Algeria in front. Karl-Heinz Rummenigge equalised. And a minute later, Lakhdar Belloumi swept in the winner.

Algeria two, West Germany one. One of the greatest shocks in World Cup history.

---

## 3. The maths  [1:55]

Algeria lost their next game, two–nil to Austria. Then they beat Chile, three–two.

Two wins from three games. Normally, that's enough to go through.

But there was a catch.

Algeria's last game finished the day before the final game in the group — West Germany against Austria.

**[Group table graphic.]**

So when the two teams walked out in Gijón, everybody knew the maths.

If West Germany won by one or two goals, West Germany and Austria would both go through. Algeria would be out.

---

## 4. The match  [2:45]

Twenty-fifth of June, 1982. El Molinón, Gijón. Forty-one thousand people.

After ten minutes, Horst Hrubesch put West Germany in front. One–nil. Exactly the result both teams needed.

And then — almost nothing happened.

For eighty minutes, the two sides passed the ball around in their own halves. Hardly a tackle. Hardly a shot. And the few shots there were flew nowhere near the goal.

**[Pitch graphic: the ball going back and forth in each team's own half.]**

The Spanish crowd turned on them. They chanted "Fuera, fuera" — "Out, out."

They chanted "Argelia, Argelia."

And then, "Que se besen." "Let them kiss."

Algerian fans in the stadium waved banknotes at the players.

On German television, the commentator Eberhard Stanjek stopped commentating altogether. On Austrian television, Robert Seeger told his viewers to turn off their sets.

The final whistle went. One–nil. West Germany through. Austria through. Algeria out.

---

## 5. The fallout  [4:15]

Algeria lodged an official protest.

FIFA looked at it, and decided that no rules had been broken. No agreement between the two teams was ever proven. The result stood.

But the Germans had a name for it anyway: the Nichtangriffspakt von Gijón. The non-aggression pact of Gijón.

The rest of the world just called it the Disgrace of Gijón.

---

## 6. The ban  [4:50]

FIFA couldn't change the result. But it could change the rules.

From the 1986 World Cup onwards, the last two games in every group kick off at exactly the same time.

No team would ever again walk out knowing exactly what result it needed.

Watching the other game first — banned. And it still is, at every World Cup, to this day.

---

## 7. What happened next  [5:25]

West Germany went all the way to the final in Madrid. They lost three–one to Italy.

Austria went out in the next round.

And Algeria went home.

But five years later, Rabah Madjer was playing for Porto, in the European Cup final — against the German champions, Bayern Munich.

Bayern were winning one–nil with twelve minutes to go. Then Madjer scored with an outrageous back-heel, and Porto won two–one.

The final was played in Vienna. In Austria.

In 2014, Algeria finally met Germany again at a World Cup. They took them all the way to extra time before losing two–one. Germany went on to win the whole tournament.

---

## 8. Forty-four years later  [6:45]

And this summer, at the 2026 World Cup, Algeria and Austria met again — in their final group game.

Both teams knew a draw might be enough. Sound familiar?

But this time, the other game in the group kicked off at exactly the same time. Nobody could do the maths in advance.

They didn't play for a draw. They played out a six-goal thriller. Three–three, with two goals in stoppage time.

And both went through. The honest way.
