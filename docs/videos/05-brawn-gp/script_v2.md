# IMPOSSIBLE: The F1 Team Bought for £1 (v2, the detailed cut)

Script for narration, about 1,650 words, roughly 11 minutes at a relaxed pace.

Read it naturally and change any wording that doesn't sound like you. [Square brackets] are notes for the edit. Don't read them out.

After a mistake, say "stop", pause, and restart the whole sentence.

What's new in v2: every technical idea is explained with a picture before we use it (the diffuser, the 2009 rules, KERS, the engine swap), plus more of the small true details that make a story feel real.

## 1. Cold open

In December 2008, a Formula One team was told it had no future.

No owner. No money. No engine. Seven hundred people about to lose their jobs.

Less than a year later, that same team was champion of the world. Both titles. In its only season.

And the secret was hidden under the back of the car, in a part most fans had never heard of.

This is the story of Brawn GP.

## 2. The end

The team at Brackley had been many things. Tyrrell. BAR. And then Honda.

Honda had spent a fortune on it. Wind tunnels, hundreds of engineers, one of the biggest budgets in the sport. And in 2008, the car was... well, it was hopeless. Jenson Button scored just three points all year.

Then the financial crisis hit. Banks were collapsing, car sales were falling off a cliff, and on the fifth of December, Honda pulled out of Formula One.

The factory was up for sale. And nobody wanted to buy it.

## 3. The car nobody had seen

But inside the factory, the team knew something nobody else did.

For 2009, Formula One was tearing up its rule book. It was the biggest change to the cars in decades.

[Graphic: 2008 car morphs into a 2009 car, each change pops in as it's named.]

The front wings got wider and lower. The rear wings got tall and skinny. All the little fins and winglets that covered the 2008 cars were banned. Slick tyres came back. And the whole point was to cut the downforce, the invisible force that pushes a car into the track, roughly in half.

Every team was starting again from a blank sheet of paper. Which meant the big teams' head start had just disappeared.

And Honda had seen it coming. They'd given up on their 2008 car early, and put everything into the 2009 one. Wind tunnels running day and night, for more than a year.

The man in charge was Ross Brawn. He was the engineer behind seven of Michael Schumacher's world titles. And he was convinced the new car was fast.

## 4. The gamble

So Ross Brawn refused to let it die. With the chief executive, Nick Fry, he took the team over himself.

The price? Reportedly, one British pound.

[Graphic: a single £1 coin spins onto a price tag.]

It wasn't quite as good a deal as it sounds. Honda helped with the bills for that first season, but there was nowhere near enough money to keep everyone. About two hundred and seventy people lost their jobs. And Jenson Button reportedly took a big pay cut, just to keep his seat.

Then there was the engine. The car had been designed around a Honda engine. And Honda were gone.

The team did a deal with Mercedes. But an engine isn't a battery you just swap over. It bolts straight onto the back of the car, and the gearbox bolts onto the engine. Different shape, different mounts, different cooling. The back of the car had to be reworked, in a matter of weeks.

[Graphic: exploded view, the Honda engine slides out, the Mercedes slides in, the gearbox and rear end re-attach.]

On the sixth of March 2009, Brawn GP was born. The first race was twenty-three days away.

## 5. The first test

Three days later, the car turned its first proper laps, at a test in Barcelona.

It went straight to the top of the timesheets.

The other teams didn't believe it. The talk in the paddock was that Brawn were running the car nearly empty of fuel. A publicity stunt, to catch the eye of a sponsor.

It wasn't a stunt. The car was almost completely white because there was hardly any money for sponsors. Just a few days before the first race, Richard Branson's Virgin signed up and their logo went on the car.

But the real reason it was so fast... was hiding underneath.

## 6. The secret: what is a diffuser?

Okay. Quick pit stop. Because "double diffuser" sounds like something you'd find in a hair salon. So what is a diffuser, and why did it matter so much?

[Technical illustration first: side cutaway of the car, airflow over and under. Then the cartoon.]

Start with the floor of the car. Most people think the wings do all the work. But a huge part of a Formula One car's grip comes from underneath.

Air rushing under the car gets squeezed into the narrow gap between the floor and the track. Squeezed air has to speed up to get through. And fast-moving air has lower pressure.

[Graphic: the air under the floor turns from blue to red as it speeds up; pressure arrows push down on top.]

So you've got normal air pressure pushing down on top of the car, and low pressure underneath. The car gets sucked onto the track. It's the same trick that keeps an aeroplane in the sky, just upside down.

More suction means more grip, and more grip means faster corners.

But there's a catch. All that fast air has to get out at the back. And if it can't escape smoothly, it chokes up, and the suction stops working.

That's the diffuser's job. It's the upswept part at the very back of the floor, the bit that curves up towards the rear wing.

[Graphic: the diffuser lights up; the flow slows and spreads out like a fan.]

It gives the air room to slow down and spread out gently. Think of it as letting the air out of the room through a wide door, instead of a cat flap. And because it's pulling air out of the back so well, it draws even more air through underneath.

The bigger the diffuser, the stronger the suction.

## 7. The double diffuser

Now remember those new rules for 2009. One of the main ways they cut downforce was to make the diffuser much smaller.

So the engineers read the rule book very, very carefully. Line by line.

And they found a gap. A gap in the wording about how the floor had to be shaped... which meant they could put a gap in the floor.

Through that opening, they fed air into a second diffuser, stacked on top of the first one.

[Graphic: the cutaway again. The hole opens, a second stream of air flows up into the upper deck. Then the cartoon double-decker.]

A double-decker diffuser. Two layers, where everyone else thought the rules allowed one. More room for the air to escape, more suction, more grip.

Some reckoned it was worth around half a second a lap. In Formula One, that's an eternity.

And there was one more thing. 2009 was also the first year of KERS, a hybrid boost button. It stored energy under braking in heavy batteries, and gave the driver a few seconds of extra power each lap.

Several of the big teams spent fortunes on it. Brawn didn't have one. Their car was lighter, simpler, and better balanced. Sometimes the smartest upgrade is the one you leave out.

## 8. The protest

Brawn weren't the only team to find the diffuser loophole. Two other teams had done it too. But Brawn had done it best, and built the whole car around it.

The rival teams were furious. They protested at the very first race.

And in April, the sport's court of appeal looked at the double diffuser and ruled that it was legal.

[Graphic: PROTESTED stamp, then LEGAL stamp slams over it.]

By then, everyone else was scrambling to copy it. But you can't just bolt a double diffuser on. The whole back of the car has to be designed around it. That took the other teams months.

And months were all Brawn needed.

## 9. The impossible

Melbourne. The twenty-ninth of March, 2009. The team's first ever race.

Jenson Button put the car on pole position. His Brazilian team-mate qualified second. [On screen: Rubens Barrichello]

On Sunday, they finished exactly where they started. First and second.

A team that hadn't existed a month earlier had just won its first race.

And Button kept winning. Malaysia, where it rained so hard the race was stopped and only half points were given. Bahrain. Spain. Monaco, where he parked in the wrong place after the race and had to jog to the podium. And Turkey.

Six wins from the first seven races.

## 10. The fight

But the money was running out. And the big teams were catching up fast.

Red Bull brought their own double diffuser. Their young German star was winning races. [On screen: Sebastian Vettel] And Brawn simply couldn't afford to keep developing the car.

There was another problem, too. The Brawn was gentle on its tyres, which sounds like a good thing. But on cold days, it couldn't get heat into them. And a cold tyre has no grip.

In the second half of the season, Button didn't win a single race. His team-mate won twice, but Button's lead shrank, race by race.

It all came down to Brazil.

Qualifying was hit by rain, and it went horribly wrong. Button would start fourteenth. His team-mate, at home in front of his own fans, was on pole.

A podium would have settled it. From fourteenth, that looked a long way off.

But this time, Button didn't need luck. He drove one of the best races of his life, passing car after car, and fought his way up to fifth. His team-mate picked up a puncture and dropped to eighth.

It was enough.

Jenson Button was world champion. And on the same day, Brawn GP won the constructors' title.

## 11. What happened next

And then, Brawn GP was gone.

At the end of the year, Mercedes bought the team. The next season, the cars were silver. And who should come out of retirement to drive one? Michael Schumacher. Back with Ross Brawn.

A few years later, Lewis Hamilton joined. And that team won eight constructors' championships in a row.

The team that was worth one pound became the most successful team of its generation.

And Brawn GP? One season. Eight wins. Two championships. A perfect record that can never be beaten.

So here's the question.

Was it the double diffuser? The car Honda left behind? Or Ross Brawn, refusing to give up?

## Fact notes (for us, not for reading)

- Honda quit 5 Dec 2008; Brawn GP announced 6 Mar 2009; Melbourne 29 Mar 2009 (23 days).
- First test: Barcelona, from 9 Mar 2009; Brawn fastest; "low fuel / sponsor stunt" talk widely reported.
- Virgin deal announced in Melbourne race week.
- About 270 redundancies (reported April 2009). Button's pay cut: widely reported, no exact figure, so "reportedly".
- 2009 rules: wider/lower front wing, taller/narrower rear wing, bodywork appendages banned, slicks back, KERS optional, diffuser height cut; aim roughly halving downforce.
- Double diffuser: Brawn, Toyota, Williams. Protested in Melbourne; FIA International Court of Appeal ruled it legal 15 Apr 2009. "Half a second a lap" is an estimate, hence "some reckoned".
- Brawn didn't run KERS (none of the Mercedes customer teams did in 2009).
- Malaysia stopped for rain, half points. Monaco: Button parked at the wrong spot and ran to the podium.
- Barrichello won Valencia and Monza. Brazil (18 Oct 2009): Barrichello pole, Button P14; result Button 5th, Barrichello 8th after a puncture.
- Brawn 2009: 8 wins (Button 6, Barrichello 2), both titles. Mercedes buy-in Nov 2009; Schumacher returned 2010; Hamilton from 2013; constructors' titles 2014–2021.
