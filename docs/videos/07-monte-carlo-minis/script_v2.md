## 1. Cold open

January 1966. Monaco.

Three little red Minis have just beaten the best rally drivers in Europe. First, second and third.

It's the third year running that a Mini has won the Monte Carlo Rally.

Then the officials take the cars apart, for hours.

And when they're done, all three are thrown out. Over their light bulbs.

This is the story of the Monte Carlo Minis. The cars that won, and went home with nothing.

## 2. The rally

The Monte Carlo Rally was the most famous motoring event in the world.

Crews set off in midwinter from cities all over Europe, from Glasgow to Athens, and drove for days to meet in Monaco.

Then came the real test. A final night in the mountains above the Riviera, against the clock, on snow and sheet ice. The most famous pass was the Col de Turini.

## 3. The giant killer

In 1964, a Northern Irishman called Paddy Hopkirk won it in a Mini Cooper S.

The Mini was a tiny family car, about ten feet long. It beat big American Fords with V8 engines.

Hopkirk came home a hero. Even the Beatles sent him a telegram.

In 1965, a Finn called Timo Mäkinen won it again, through a blizzard. More than two hundred cars started. Only thirty-five made it to the finish.

## 4. How a Mini beat a V8

So how does a car that small beat cars with twice the power?

[Animated cutaway of the Mini from above: each part lights up as it's named.]

First, the engine. The Mini's engine sat sideways at the front, driving the front wheels. So all its weight sat over the wheels that were doing the pulling. On ice, that's a huge advantage.

Second, the wheels were pushed right out to the corners. Tiny ten-inch wheels, with almost nothing hanging over the ends. It went round corners like a go-kart.

Third, it was light. The big Fords weighed nearly twice as much, and on a frozen mountain road, all that power is no use if the tyres just spin.

And the Finns had a trick of their own. Left-foot braking.

[Diagram: two feet on the pedals. Right foot on the throttle, left foot on the brake, the car tucking into the bend.]

Instead of lifting off the throttle for a corner, they kept the power on with the right foot, and dabbed the brake with the left. It kept the engine pulling and tucked the nose into the bend.

Rauno Aaltonen is usually credited as the man who perfected it. Today, almost every rally driver does it.

## 5. The new rules

For 1966, the rules changed.

Cars that had been modified for competition would now carry a heavy handicap on their times.

The way to win was with a standard car. One that had been built at least five thousand times, to the same spec.

So BMC built and sold five thousand Cooper S models to qualify.

But there was a catch, hidden in the small print.

On a standard car, the lights had to be standard too.

## 6. The run

The works team sent its red Minis with white roofs. Mäkinen, Hopkirk, and another flying Finn, Rauno Aaltonen.

On the final night in the mountains, nobody could touch them.

Mäkinen won. Aaltonen was second. Hopkirk was third.

Roger Clark's Lotus Cortina was fourth.

The Citroën of Pauli Toivonen was only fifth.

The crews celebrated in Monaco. A hat-trick of wins, and a clean sweep of the podium.

## 7. The longest night

Then the scrutineers moved in.

The cars were taken into a garage, and the doors were shut.

They stripped the Minis down. Engines, gearboxes, suspension. Hopkirk later said they even counted the teeth on the gears.

Outside, the crews waited. Hour after hour.

Some accounts say the checks took eight hours. Others say the best part of a day.

The engines were legal. The gearboxes were legal.

In the end, it came down to the lights.

## 8. How the lights worked

[Animated headlight cutaway: two bulbs side by side.]

A normal headlight bulb of the time had two filaments inside it. One for main beam. And a second one, set slightly off-centre, for dipped beam. Flick the switch, and the light drops down, out of the eyes of the driver coming the other way.

[The dipped filament glows, the beam drops.]

The Minis were using new quartz iodine bulbs. Much brighter than normal ones, which matters a lot on a mountain at three in the morning.

But those bulbs had a single filament. They couldn't dip.

[The single-filament bulb: one beam, straight ahead.]

So when another car came the other way, the crews switched their main lamps off and their other lamps on.

The rules for standard cars said the headlights had to dip the way the factory built them.

BMC said the same bulbs had been used on the Monte for two years without a word.

It was said the British teams never saw the final version of that rule. The French teams reportedly did.

## 9. Thrown out

Then the result came out.

All three Minis were disqualified.

Clark's Cortina was out too. So was Rosemary Smith's Hillman Imp, which had won the ladies' prize.

In all, ten cars were thrown out.

## 10. The winner nobody wanted

The new winner was a Citroën DS21, driven by Pauli Toivonen.

His Citroën reportedly had iodine lamps as well. But on some DS models they came fitted from the factory, so they were allowed.

Toivonen was said to be embarrassed by it all. In the newsreels, he collects his trophy looking deeply unimpressed.

Monaco's Prince Rainier was unhappy too. He left before the prize-giving.

## 11. The backfire

The organisers had their result. But they lost the argument.

Back in Britain, one magazine simply called it "The Monte Carlo Fiasco".

The disqualified Minis reportedly ended up on stage at the London Palladium, on the biggest TV show in the country.

Hopkirk said the row gave the Mini ten times as much publicity as a win would have.

BMC appealed. In October 1966, the disqualifications were upheld.

A year later, in 1967, Rauno Aaltonen won the Monte Carlo Rally. In a Mini.

## 12. What happened next

Timo Mäkinen won Finland's Thousand Lakes Rally four times, and Britain's RAC Rally three years in a row.

Paddy Hopkirk was made an MBE in 2016.

Rauno Aaltonen, the man they called the Rally Professor, kept on rallying into the 1980s.

Pauli Toivonen became European Rally Champion in 1968, in a Porsche.

Twenty years after his awkward win, his son Henri won the Monte Carlo Rally too. Pauli reportedly said the family name had finally been cleared.

And the Mini? More than five million of the originals were built, until the year 2000.
