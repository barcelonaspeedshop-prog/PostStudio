## 1. Cold open

January 1966. Monaco.

Three little red Minis have just beaten the best rally drivers in Europe. First, second and third.

It's the third year running that a Mini has won the Monte Carlo Rally.

Then the officials take the cars apart, for hours.

And when they're done, all three are thrown out. Over their headlights. [STAMP: DISQUALIFIED]

This is the story of the Monte Carlo Minis. The cars that won, and went home with nothing.

## 2. The giant killer

The Monte Carlo Rally was the most famous motoring event in the world.

Crews set off in midwinter from all over Europe, and drove for days to reach Monaco.

The last night was the hardest. Snow and ice, on mountain passes high above the Riviera, like the Col de Turini.

In 1964, a Northern Irishman called Paddy Hopkirk won it in a Mini Cooper S.

The Mini was a tiny family car, about ten feet long. It beat big American Fords with V8 engines.

Hopkirk came home a hero. Even the Beatles sent him a telegram.

In 1965, a Finn called Timo Mäkinen won it again, through a blizzard. More than two hundred cars started. Only thirty-five made it to the finish.

## 3. The new rules

For 1966, the rules changed.

Cars that had been modified for competition would now carry a heavy handicap on their times.

The way to win was with a standard car. One that had been built at least five thousand times, to the same spec.

So BMC built and sold five thousand Cooper S models to qualify.

But there was a catch, hidden in the small print.

On a standard car, the lights had to be standard too.

## 4. The run

The works team sent its red Minis with white roofs. Mäkinen, Hopkirk, and another flying Finn, Rauno Aaltonen.

On the final night in the mountains, nobody could touch the Minis.

Mäkinen won. Aaltonen was second. Hopkirk was third.

Roger Clark's Lotus Cortina was fourth.

The Citroën of Pauli Toivonen was only fifth.

## 5. The panic

That's when the scrutineers moved in.

They stripped the Minis down. Hopkirk later said they even counted the teeth on the gears.

Some accounts say the checks took eight hours. Others say the best part of a day.

In the end, it came down to the lights.

## 6. Thrown out

The Minis were using new quartz iodine bulbs, much brighter than normal ones.

But those bulbs had a single filament. They couldn't dip.

When another car came the other way, the crews dipped by switching over to their other lamps instead.

The rules for standard cars said the lights should dip the way the factory built them, with an ordinary twin-filament bulb.

BMC said the same bulbs had been used on the Monte for two years without a word.

It was said the British teams never saw the final version of that rule. The French teams reportedly did.

All three Minis were disqualified. [STAMP: DISQUALIFIED]

Clark's Cortina was out too. So was Rosemary Smith's Hillman Imp, which had won the ladies' prize.

In all, ten cars were thrown out. [STAMP: 10 CARS DISQUALIFIED]

## 7. The winner nobody wanted

The new winner was a Citroën DS21, driven by Pauli Toivonen.

His Citroën reportedly had iodine lamps as well. But on some DS models, they came fitted from the factory, so they were allowed.

Toivonen was said to be embarrassed by it all. In the newsreels, he collects his trophy looking deeply unimpressed.

Monaco's Prince Rainier was unhappy too. He left before the prize-giving.

## 8. The backfire

The organisers had their result. But they lost the argument.

Back in Britain, one magazine simply called it "The Monte Carlo Fiasco".

The disqualified Minis reportedly ended up on stage at the London Palladium, on the biggest TV show in the country.

Hopkirk said the row gave the Mini ten times as much publicity as a win would have.

BMC appealed. In October 1966, the disqualifications were upheld. [STAMP: APPEAL REJECTED]

A year later, in 1967, Rauno Aaltonen won the Monte Carlo Rally. In a Mini.

## 9. What happened next

Timo Mäkinen won Finland's Thousand Lakes Rally four times, and Britain's RAC Rally three years in a row. He died in 2017.

Paddy Hopkirk was made an MBE in 2016, and died in 2022, aged eighty-nine.

Rauno Aaltonen, the man they called the Rally Professor, kept on rallying into the 1980s.

Pauli Toivonen became European Rally Champion in 1968, in a Porsche.

Twenty years after his awkward win, his son Henri won the Monte Carlo Rally too. Pauli reportedly said the family name had finally been cleared.

That May, Henri and his co-driver, Sergio Cresto, were killed on the Tour de Corse, in the final season of Group B. That's a story we told in our Group B episode. [Card: link to BANNED: Group B]

And the Mini? More than five million of the originals were built, until the year 2000.
