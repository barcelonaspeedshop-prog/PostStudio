## 1. Cold open

In 1995, a road car lined up at the start of the Le Mans 24 Hours.

Its designer had never wanted it to race. It had never run for twenty-four hours. And it was up against prototypes built for nothing but to win this race.

Twenty-four hours later, the car that shouldn't have won it had won it. At the first attempt.

This is the story of the McLaren F1.

## 2. The road car

In the early nineties, Gordon Murray set out to build the greatest road car ever made.

Three seats, with the driver in the middle. A six-litre BMW V12. This thing even had gold foil lining the engine bay, because that was the best material to reflect heat.

It was the fastest production car in the world, and Murray was clear about one thing. It was a road car. It was not going racing.

## 3. The customers

But the people who bought McLaren F1s were not the type to take no for an answer.

Some of them wanted to race. And they told McLaren they would race it, with or without the factory's help.

So McLaren gave in, and built a racing version. The F1 GTR.

It won races straight away, in the sports car series of 1995. But Le Mans? Well, that was a different story altogether.

## 4. The mountain

Le Mans is twenty-four hours, flat out. It breaks the best-prepared cars in the world.

The fastest cars there were prototypes. Purpose-built racing machines, lower, lighter, and designed for one job: to win the world's most famous endurance race.

The McLarens had never run for twenty-four hours. Not once. Not even in testing.

One of them, number fifty-nine, was the oldest of the lot. The original test car. Painted plain grey, run by a small, Japanese-backed private team.

Its drivers were Yannick Dalmas, JJ Lehto and Masanori Sekiya. The line-up certainly had what it took to do the job. But did their untested race car?

## 5. The rain

Saturday, four o'clock. The race started in the dry, and the little WR prototypes, which had locked out the front row, pulled away from the field. The McLarens were down in the chasing pack.

Then, at the end of the first hour, the rain came. And it didn't stop.

For around sixteen hours, the circuit was soaked. Cars aquaplaned off the track in the dark. The light prototypes struggled, and the heavy McLarens came to the front.

JJ Lehto was on another level. In the middle of the night, in the pouring rain, he was reportedly lapping as much as twenty seconds faster than anyone else on the track.

But number fifty-nine still wasn't leading. Another McLaren led for over eight hours, until its clutch broke at three in the morning. Then the Harrods McLaren of Derek Bell, his son Justin and Andy Wallace took over at the front, with the grey car chasing.

## 6. The impossible

As the sun came up on Sunday, the rain finally eased. Number fifty-nine was second, with the Harrods car just ahead.

Then, in the final hours, the Harrods car's gearbox started to fail. At one pit stop, it lost five crucial minutes just trying to get into gear.

Yannick Dalmas swept past into the lead.

At four o'clock in the afternoon, number fifty-nine crossed the line to win the Le Mans 24 Hours, three quarters of a lap ahead of the best prototype in the race.

And it wasn't alone. McLarens finished first, third, fourth and fifth.

A road car, built for the road, had beaten the purpose-built racers at the toughest race in the world. At the first attempt.

## 7. What happened next

The win gave McLaren something no other team has managed. The Triple Crown. Victory at the Monaco Grand Prix, the Indianapolis 500, and the Le Mans 24 Hours.

Gordon Murray later said that winning Le Mans was harder than winning a Formula One championship. He should know. He'd done both.

Only one hundred and six McLaren F1s were ever built, racing cars included. Today, they're some of the most valuable cars on earth.

And number fifty-nine, the grey test car that won Le Mans, is still the most famous of them all.
