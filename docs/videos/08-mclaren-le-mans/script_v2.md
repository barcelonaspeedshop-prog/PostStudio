## 1. Cold open

June 1995. Le Mans.

A road car lines up for the hardest race in the world.

Its designer never wanted it to race. It has never run for twenty-four hours, not even in testing. And it's up against prototypes, built for one thing only: to win this race.

Twenty-four hours later, it has won it. At the first attempt.

This is the story of the McLaren F1.

## 2. The idea

In 1988, Gordon Murray was the most famous designer in Formula One. His McLaren had just won fifteen of the sixteen races.

On the way home from the Italian Grand Prix, waiting for a flight, he reportedly sketched a road car. A car with three seats, and the driver in the middle.

He wanted it to be the greatest road car ever made. No compromise. Light, fast, and easy to drive every day.

## 3. How the F1 was built

[Animated cutaway of the F1 from above, then from the side: each part lights up as it's named.]

So what made it so different?

First, the seat. The driver sat right in the middle, like in a racing car. A passenger sat on each side, just behind. You got a perfect view of the road, and the car's weight was balanced around you.

Second, the body. The F1 was the first road car built around a carbon fibre tub, the way Formula One cars are. Very strong, and very light. The whole car weighed about eleven hundred kilos.

Third, the engine. A six-litre BMW V12, with more than six hundred horsepower, sitting right behind the driver.

And then there was the gold.

[Close-up: the engine bay, the gold lining glowing.]

The engine bay was lined with real gold foil. Not for show. Gold is one of the best materials there is for reflecting heat, and that V12 made a lot of heat.

In 1998, an F1 hit two hundred and forty miles an hour. For seven years, no production car went faster.

And Murray was clear about one thing. It was a road car. It was not going racing.

## 4. The customers

But the people who bought McLaren F1s were not the type to take no for an answer.

Some of them wanted to race. And they told McLaren they would race it, with or without the factory's help.

So McLaren gave in, and built a racing version. The F1 GTR.

In 1995, it won race after race in the GT series. But Le Mans? That was a different story altogether.

## 5. The mountain

Le Mans is twenty-four hours, flat out. Day, night, and day again. It breaks the best-prepared cars in the world.

The fastest cars there were prototypes. Purpose-built racing machines, lower and lighter, with no road car anywhere in their family tree.

The McLarens had never run for twenty-four hours. Not once. Nobody knew whether the gearbox would last.

One of them, number fifty-nine, was the oldest of the lot. The original test car. Painted grey, run by a small, Japanese-backed private team.

Its drivers were Yannick Dalmas, a Frenchman who had already won Le Mans. JJ Lehto, a Finn who had driven for Benetton in Formula One. And Masanori Sekiya, from Japan.

On Saturday afternoon, the race started in the dry. The little prototypes had locked out the front row, and they pulled away.

The McLarens were down in the chasing pack.

## 6. The rain

Then, at the end of the first hour, the rain came. And it didn't stop.

For around sixteen hours, the circuit was soaked. In the dark, at close to two hundred miles an hour.

[Animated explainer: a tyre from the side, rolling into a film of water.]

Here's why that changed everything.

When a tyre hits standing water, the water has to get out of the way. The grooves in the tyre squeeze it out to the sides.

But go fast enough, and the water can't escape in time. It builds up in a wedge in front of the tyre, and the tyre rides up on top of it. That's aquaplaning. No grip at all.

[The wedge grows; the tyre lifts.]

The more weight pressing a tyre down, the harder it can squeeze through the water.

The prototypes were light. Brilliant in the dry, but nervous in the wet.

The McLaren was a heavier car. A road car, with a roof and a windscreen. And now it came to the front.

JJ Lehto was on another level. In the middle of the night, in the pouring rain, he was reportedly lapping as much as twenty seconds faster than almost anyone else on the track.

But number fifty-nine still wasn't leading. Another McLaren led for hours, until its clutch broke in the early hours of Sunday.

Then the Harrods McLaren took over at the front. Derek Bell, a five-time Le Mans winner, his son Justin, and Andy Wallace.

The grey car was chasing.

## 7. The last hours

As the sun came up on Sunday, the rain finally eased.

Number fifty-nine was second. The Harrods car was just ahead.

Behind them, a prototype was closing in. The Courage of Bob Wollek, Éric Hélary, and the American racing legend, Mario Andretti.

Then, in the final hours, the Harrods car's gearbox started to fail. At one pit stop, it lost precious minutes just trying to find a gear.

The grey car didn't need asking twice.

Yannick Dalmas swept past into the lead.

Now it was all about the gearbox in number fifty-nine. One more failure, one more missed gear, and it was over.

The minutes ticked down.

At four o'clock in the afternoon, the grey McLaren crossed the line. It had won the Le Mans 24 Hours.

In the pit lane, the mechanics jumped the barrier. The crowd poured onto the track.

And it wasn't alone. McLarens finished first, third, fourth and fifth.

A road car, built for the road, had beaten the purpose-built racers at the toughest race in the world. At the first attempt.

## 8. What happened next

Masanori Sekiya became the first Japanese driver ever to win Le Mans.

Yannick Dalmas went on to win it four times in all.

The win gave McLaren something no other team has managed. The Triple Crown. Victory at the Monaco Grand Prix, the Indianapolis 500, and the Le Mans 24 Hours.

Only one hundred and six McLaren F1s were ever built, racing cars included. In 2021, a road car sold for more than twenty million dollars.

And number fifty-nine, the grey test car that won Le Mans, is still the most famous of them all.

## Fact notes (for us, not for reading)

- **1988:** McLaren won 15 of 16 GPs (MP4/4, Murray chief designer). Murray's three-seat sketch is reportedly from waiting for a flight home after the 1988 Italian GP ("reportedly").
- **The car:** F1 road car launched 1992. Central driving seat, 3 seats. First production road car with a carbon-fibre monocoque. About 1,140 kg ("about eleven hundred kilos"). BMW S70/2 6.1-litre V12, 627 PS. Gold-foil heat lining in the engine bay.
- **Top speed:** 240.1 mph at Ehra-Lessien, March 1998 (Andy Wallace driving), the production-car record for years.
- **The race:** 1995 BPR wins for the F1 GTR. At Le Mans, WR prototypes took the front row. Rain from early in the race, heavy overnight. #59 was chassis 01R, the development car, run by Kokusai Kaihatsu Racing (Lanzante ran the car), grey with Ueno Clinic logos.
- **Who led:** the Gulf/GTC McLaren led overnight until its clutch failed. The Harrods #51 (Bell/Bell/Wallace) led into Sunday, then had gearbox trouble and finished 3rd. Courage C34 (Wollek/Hélary/Andretti) finished 2nd. McLarens finished 1st, 3rd, 4th and 5th.
- **The drivers:** Lehto drove for Benetton in 1994. Dalmas won Le Mans 1992, 1994, 1995 and 1999. Sekiya was the first Japanese winner.
- **Afterwards:** Triple Crown = Monaco GP, Indy 500 (McLaren cars 1974, 1976) and Le Mans 1995. 106 F1s built in all. 2021: a road car sold for $20.465m.
- **Aquaplaning:** a simplified explainer. Tyre load helps, but so do grooves and speed. Say "can", not "always".
- **Removed from v1:** "three quarters of a lap ahead" (not sure of the margin), and Murray's "harder than an F1 title" line (couldn't confirm it).
