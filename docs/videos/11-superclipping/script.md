# F1 Explained: Superclipping, the rule F1 had to fix

Omnira F1 long video, about 1,450 words, roughly 9 minutes. AI narration: **Malachi** (modern F1, eleven_v3, the model of his sample). [Square brackets] are notes for the edit and aren't read out.

**Release:** Thursday 29 October, 7pm (Spain), Omnira F1. Teaser Short Wednesday 28 at 7pm.

**Names spoken:** Bearman (BEER-mun), Colapinto (co-la-PIN-toe), Tombazis (tom-BAH-zis), Stella.

## 1. Cold open

Bahrain, February 2026. A brand-new Formula One car comes out of the fastest corner on the track, flat out. The driver's foot is pinned to the floor.

And the car is slowing down.

[Speed readout: 267 falling to 233 km/h while a throttle bar stays at 100%.]

Thirty kilometres an hour, gone. Not because he lifted. Because the car decided to.

Fernando Alonso joked that now even the team chef could drive it.

Within three months, Formula One had to change its own brand-new rules. This is superclipping, explained.

## 2. Half a battery car

[Chapter: THE NEW CARS]

To understand superclipping, you need to know what changed in 2026.

Formula One threw out its old engines. The new ones get about half their power from a petrol engine, and about half from an electric motor and a battery.

[Graphic: a power bar splitting roughly 50/50, petrol and electric.]

That electric motor is huge: three hundred and fifty kilowatts. Close to five hundred horsepower, from electricity alone.

But the battery is small. It only holds enough energy for a few seconds at full power.

[The battery gauge drains in a few seconds on a straight.]

So it has to be refilled. Again and again, every single lap.

## 3. Refilling on the move

[Chapter: HOW THE BATTERY GETS REFILLED]

The old cars had two ways to make electricity. One from the brakes, and one from the hot exhaust gases spinning a turbo.

For 2026, the exhaust one was scrapped. Too expensive, too complicated.

[Graphic: the old car with two generators; the exhaust one is crossed out.]

That left one. The motor on the rear axle can run backwards, as a generator. When it does, it slows the car down, and turns that energy into electricity.

[Animation: the motor spins backwards, energy arrows flow into the battery.]

Under braking, that's perfect. You want to slow down anyway.

But on many tracks, there isn't enough braking. Long straights, fast corners, and not enough heavy stops to fill the battery back up.

## 4. Lift, or clip

[Chapter: THE PROBLEM]

So the cars have to find energy somewhere else. There are two ways.

The first is lift and coast. The driver lifts off the throttle before the braking zone, and the motor harvests while the car rolls.

[Lift-and-coast graphic: throttle trace drops to zero before the braking point.]

But in 2026, that has a catch. The new cars have moving wings. On the straights, they flatten out to cut drag. Lift off the throttle, and they flip back up into corner mode.

[Active aero animation: the wings flatten for the straight, then snap up when the throttle trace drops.]

That costs speed you can't get back.

So there's a second way. Keep your foot flat to the floor, keep the wings flat, and let the motor harvest anyway.

That's clipping. And when the motor harvests hard, at full throttle, through the end of a straight or a fast corner, that's superclipping.

[Speed trace: two lines, one lifting and coasting, one superclipping, both slowing before the braking point.]

The car runs out of push before the driver ever touches the brake.

## 5. What it looked like

[Chapter: WHAT IT LOOKED LIKE]

At the first tests, the limit for superclipping was two hundred and fifty kilowatts.

At Bahrain's fastest corner, that was enough to take the top speed from two hundred and sixty-seven kilometres an hour down to two hundred and thirty-three. With the driver flat out.

[Speed readout again, beside the Bahrain track map with Turn 12 lit.]

On television, it looked strange. A car at full throttle, getting slower. Its engine note dropping away halfway down the straight.

And in Melbourne, analysis suggested cars needed around sixteen seconds of superclipping on every lap, just to keep the battery going.

[Lap map of Melbourne with the superclipping zones glowing.]

Sixteen seconds a lap, at full throttle, getting slower.

## 6. The danger

[Chapter: THE DANGER]

That was strange. But then it became dangerous.

Because not every car uses its energy in the same place. One car can have far more energy to deploy than the car in front. One pushing hard, one holding back, on the same straight.

[Two-car diagram: one car's speed rising, the other falling, the gap closing fast.]

At the Japanese Grand Prix, at Suzuka, Oliver Bearman's Haas came up behind Franco Colapinto's Alpine.

Bearman was using his battery. The difference in speed was about fifty kilometres an hour.

[Closing-speed graphic: 50 km/h gap.]

He had nowhere to go. The Haas went off and hit the barrier. The impact was measured at fifty G.

[Impact readout: 50G.]

Bearman walked away with bruising. But drivers said they had seen it coming.

## 7. The fix

[Chapter: THE FIX]

The FIA, the teams and the engine makers met in April.

And before the Miami Grand Prix, they changed the rules. All the teams agreed.

[Rule card: SUPERCLIPPING LIMIT 250 kW → 350 kW. QUALIFYING ENERGY 8 MJ → 7 MJ.]

The superclipping limit went up, from two hundred and fifty kilowatts to three hundred and fifty.

That sounds backwards. Harvest harder? But it means the car fills its battery in a shorter burst, instead of a long, slow fade down the whole straight. Less time slowing, and less need to lift and coast.

[Speed trace: a long gentle fade becomes a short, sharp dip.]

And in qualifying, the energy cars could recharge each lap came down, from eight megajoules to seven. So drivers could push harder for more of the lap.

## 8. Did it work?

[Chapter: DID IT WORK?]

The answer depends on who you ask.

Max Verstappen called the changes more of a tickle. Not what was needed to make the cars truly flat out.

Lewis Hamilton said the Sunday racing was the best of his career.

[Two quote cards: Verstappen, Hamilton.]

And Oscar Piastri warned that the gap in speed between two cars could still be huge.

[Quote card: Piastri.]

What everyone agrees on is this. In 2026, the fastest driver isn't always the one with the best car through the corners. It's the one who spends his energy best.

## 9. How to spot it

[Chapter: HOW TO SPOT IT]

So next time you watch a race, look for it.

Watch a car at the end of a long straight. Listen to the engine note. If it drops away before the braking zone, while the driver is still flat out, that's superclipping.

[Onboard-style graphic: the throttle bar at 100%, the speed number falling, the word SUPERCLIPPING.]

You're watching a car refill its battery at three hundred kilometres an hour.

## Fact notes (for us, not for reading)

- **2026 power units:** MGU-H removed; roughly 50/50 split between combustion engine and electrical power; MGU-K up to 350 kW (about 470 hp). Battery energy per lap limited (qualifying recharge 8 MJ, cut to 7 MJ from Miami). Sources: The Race, Motorsport.com, Speedcafe.
- **Superclipping:** MGU-K harvesting at full throttle (end of straights, fast corners); keeps the active aero in straight mode; lift-and-coast harvesting puts the wings into corner mode. Pre-Miami limit 250 kW (braking/lift-and-coast up to 350 kW). Sources: The Race, Racer, Motorsport.com.
