## 1. Cold open

At half-time in the 2005 Champions League final, Liverpool were losing three–nil against AC Milan, one of the best teams in the world.

Nobody comes back from that. Not in a final, and definitely not against Milan.

Fifteen minutes into the second half, the score was three–three.

And the reason started with a change at half-time that most people in the stadium never even noticed.

This is the story of Liverpool's miracle in Istanbul.

## 2. The outsiders

Liverpool shouldn't really have been there in the first place.

They had a new Spanish manager, Rafa Benítez [the new Spanish manager], in his first season at the club.

In the Premier League, they finished fifth. Not even good enough to qualify for the next season's Champions League.

And in Europe, they nearly went out before Christmas.

In their last group game, at Anfield against the Greek champions, Olympiacos, they had to win. And because they'd lost one–nil in Greece, if the Greeks scored, Liverpool would have to win by two clear goals.

Then a Brazilian World Cup winner, Rivaldo, curled in a free kick. At half-time, Liverpool were losing one–nil. Now they needed three goals in forty-five minutes.

Benítez sent on a young French striker. Two minutes after the restart, he equalised. One–one.

[On screen: Florent Sinama-Pongolle]

With nine minutes left, another substitute, Neil Mellor, made it two–one. But that still wasn't enough.

Then, with four minutes to go, Mellor headed the ball down, and Steven Gerrard hit it first time from twenty-five yards, into the corner.

Three–one. Liverpool were through. Looking back, it was a sign of things to come.

After that, they knocked out a German side, then the Italian giants Juventus, then Chelsea.

And somehow, they were in the final.

## 3. The giants

Waiting for them in Istanbul was a team that can only be described as a European football Goliath. AC Milan.

Champions of Europe two years earlier, and packed with world-class players.

Maldini and Nesta at the back, two of the best defenders on the planet. Pirlo, who could pass the ball anywhere he wanted. Kaká, the young Brazilian who would become the best player in the world. And up front, Crespo, and the European Footballer of the Year, Andriy Shevchenko.

The final was at the national stadium, out on the edge of the city, on the twenty-fifth of May, 2005.

It was so far out that many fans had to walk the last part, along a dusty road, past fields.

Around forty thousand Liverpool fans made the trip. Liverpool in red. Milan in white.

## 4. The first half

It took Milan less than a minute to score.

A free kick came in, and the Milan captain, Paolo Maldini, volleyed it home. About fifty seconds on the clock.

It's still the fastest goal ever scored in a Champions League final.

Then Liverpool lost Harry Kewell to injury. A Czech midfielder, Vladimír Šmicer [a Czech midfielder], came on in his place. Remember him.

Six minutes before the break, Shevchenko crossed, and Crespo tapped it in. Two–nil.

Right on half-time, Kaká split the Liverpool defence with one pass. Crespo chipped the goalkeeper.

Three–nil to Milan at the break.

## 5. What was going wrong

So what was going wrong? It wasn't just bad luck.

[Pitch diagram. Liverpool's four defenders in a line, four midfielders in front, and a gap between them that lights up.]

Liverpool were playing four at the back, with four in midfield. And between those two lines, there was a gap.

That gap was where Kaká lived.

He'd drift into it, turn, and run at the defence, or slip a pass through it. Every time he got the ball there, Liverpool were in trouble.

Milan's midfield was full of passers too. Pirlo sat deep and sprayed the ball around, and Liverpool couldn't get near him.

Too much space, and too much time. Against players this good, that's fatal.

## 7. The change

In the dressing room, Benítez didn't panic. He changed the plan.

And it nearly went a different way. He was going to take off the left-back, Djimi Traoré [the left-back]. Traoré was reportedly already heading for the showers.

But then the physio told him the right-back, Steve Finnan, was injured. So Finnan came off instead, and Traoré had to get changed again.

On came Didi Hamann [a German midfielder].

[Pitch diagram again. The back four becomes a back three, Hamann drops into the gap, Gerrard moves forward.]

Here's what that did.

Liverpool went to three at the back. Carragher, Hyypiä [the big Finnish centre-back] and Traoré.

Hamann sat right in front of them, in exactly the space Kaká had been using. Every time Kaká turned, Hamann was there.

And with Hamann doing the defending, Gerrard was free to push forward and join the attack.

The wide players became wing-backs, running up and down the whole side of the pitch.

Liverpool still couldn't stop Milan passing. But now there was no gap to pass into. And Gerrard was suddenly much closer to their goal.

Meanwhile, up in the stands, the Liverpool fans did something remarkable.

They didn't boo. They sang. "You'll Never Walk Alone."

Some of the players say they could hear it from the dressing room. The manager says he only heard it on the way back out.

Either way, the fans hadn't given up. So why should the team?

## 8. Six minutes

Nine minutes into the second half, Liverpool's left-back, John Arne Riise [the left-back], crossed from the left.

Gerrard rose and headed it in. Three–one.

He jogged back to the halfway line waving his arms at the crowd. Come on. Make some noise.

Two minutes later, Šmicer hit a low shot from outside the box. It skidded past the Milan keeper, Dida. Three–two.

Then Gerrard burst into the box, exactly where the new shape had put him, and Gattuso brought him down. Penalty.

Xabi Alonso [Liverpool's Spanish midfielder] stepped up. The keeper saved it.

But the ball came straight back to him, and he smashed in the rebound.

Three goals in six minutes. Three–three.

## 9. The save

After that, Milan took control again. Liverpool clung on into extra time, with players going down with cramp all over the pitch.

With three minutes left, the ball fell to Shevchenko, right in front of goal.

Liverpool's Polish goalkeeper, Jerzy Dudek, blocked his header.

The ball came straight back to Shevchenko, from even closer.

Somehow, Dudek threw up a hand, and it flew over the bar.

He later called it the save of his career.

## 10. Penalties

And so the game was going to penalties.

Before the shoot-out, Jamie Carragher reportedly jumped on his goalkeeper's back with some advice.

Put them off, he said. Do what Bruce Grobbelaar [Liverpool's keeper back in 1984] did in Rome in 1984, when Liverpool won the European Cup on penalties.

So Dudek danced on his line. Wobbly legs. Arms everywhere.

Milan's first taker blazed it over the bar.

Hamann scored. And he took that penalty, reportedly, with a broken bone in his foot.

Then Pirlo stepped up, and Dudek saved it.

Both teams scored. Then Dida saved one from Riise. Then both teams scored again, Šmicer included.

Which left Shevchenko. The European Footballer of the Year. He had to score, or Milan were finished.

Dudek dived, and stopped it with his left hand.

Liverpool were champions of Europe.

## 11. What happened next

It was Liverpool's fifth European Cup. Under the rules of the time, that meant they kept the trophy for good.

They were the last club ever to win the original permanently.

But there was a problem. Liverpool's fifth place in the league meant they weren't in next season's Champions League.

UEFA made an exception. The holders could defend their title, but they had to start in the very first qualifying round.

And Šmicer never played for Liverpool again. That penalty in Istanbul was his last kick for the club.

Two years later, the same two teams met in the final again, this time in Athens.

Milan won it, two–one.

But in Liverpool, one word still means more than any other final. Istanbul.

## 12. What if

So here's the question.

What if Benítez had taken Traoré off instead of Finnan? What if that save in extra time doesn't happen?
